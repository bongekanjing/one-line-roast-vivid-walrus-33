# Drafts

Eighteen candidates, each invented here. Character counts are the exact length of the line as
one UTF-8 string with no trailing newline. Every candidate is scored against the judge's five
criteria in order: (1) names a daily gadget or app, (2) lands on a first read, (3) fresh angle,
(4) tight wording, (5) one person's concrete gripe about how the thing behaves.

## Candidates

1. **122** — `My watch counts standing by the arm, not the legs, so I chop onions one-handed and let the other arm hang like an apology.`
   Passes 1 (smartwatch, named behaviourally). 2: yes — the reader knows the stand ring and feels
   caught. 3: fresh — nobody roasts the stand detection as an arm-angle sensor. 4: tight, two
   clauses, no filler. 5: very concrete — chopping onions, one hand, a specific wrist.

2. **122** — `My watch won't count standing with my arm on the counter, so I do the dishes one-handed and let the other wrist apologize.`
   Same mechanical insight, weaker staging: "do the dishes" is generic housework where "chop
   onions" is a specific posture. Also "the other wrist" repeats "arm" less cleanly than the
   final clause in 1.

3. **124** — `My smartwatch won't count standing unless my arm hangs down, so I cook with my wrist dangling at the kettle like an apology.`
   Good 3 and 5, but "unless my arm hangs down" states the rule flatly; 1 states the absurdity
   of the rule and then shows it. "Cook with my wrist dangling at the kettle" is also one image
   too many for one clause.

4. **108** — `My earbuds connect to the laptop two rooms away and refuse the phone in my pocket: impressive range, no aim.`
   Passes 1, 2 (the pun lands), 4. Fails 3 badly — "earbuds connect to the wrong device" is the
   obvious complaint about every pair of Bluetooth buds. Only 5 saves it, and 3 is weighed first.

5. **125** — `My banking app wants a fingerprint, a PIN, and a code texted to the phone in my hand, to show me a balance of eleven dollars.`
   Passes 1, 2, 5. 3 is thin — "the bank app asks for too much" is a standard grumble; only the
   "eleven dollars" sting is new, and that is a small payload for 125 characters.

6. **128** — `My smart TV loads the app I pay for in forty seconds and the trailer for something I don't in one, so I know which one it likes.`
   Passes 1, 2, 4. 3 is close to the familiar "my TV is full of ads" line; the timed comparison
   is the only fresh part. 5 is decent but the detail is a stopwatch, not a behaviour.

7. **116** — `The food app said two minutes for nineteen minutes, so now I open it just to watch a man orbit my street on a moped.`
   Passes 1, 2, 4, 5. 3 weak — "the ETA is a lie" is the most repeated delivery-app joke there is,
   including "he's circling the block". The final image is good but the setup is reheated.

8. **106** — `My printer jams only on the page with my signature on it, which is less a fault than a considered opinion.`
   Passes 1, 2, 4, 5. 3 fails on the brief's own example: "printer jams" is the obvious complaint,
   and the "considered opinion" framing, while neat, is a well-worn personification move.

9. **119** — `My kettle installed a firmware update this morning; the water boiled exactly as well, but the kettle now knows my name.`
   Passes 1, 2, 4. 3 is decent — firmware on a kettle is the exact absurdity of the category — but
   the ending drifts into generic surveillance humour that could be about any appliance.

10. **131** — `The e-scooter charged me six dollars to travel four hundred metres, then praised the thirty-four calories I burned walking it back.`
    Passes 1, 2, 5 strongly; 3 is good. Fails 4 — three numbers in 131 characters is one detail too
    many, and "thirty-four calories" spends eleven characters to make a joke already made by
    "walking it back".

11. **126** — `The parking app takes longer to log in than the meter takes to eat my coins, so I pay both and stand there feeling supervised.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4, 5. 3 is mild — "the app is slower than the thing it replaced" is the standard
    parking-app complaint, and "feeling supervised" is a mood, not a behaviour.

12. **121** — `My password manager generates a thirty-character password and then refuses to fill it in on the one site it was made for.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4. 3 is thin; 5 is the strongest part (a password manager failing on exactly one
    site) but the line never says which site or what happened, so the detail stays abstract.

13. **124** — `My phone's assistant apologizes before I finish asking, which means it hears the sigh perfectly and the question not at all.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4. 3 strained — "my assistant is useless" is the obvious complaint, and the
    apology-before-the-question trick is a known demo failure. 5 is a mood, not an observation.

14. **113** — `My keyboard corrects 'tomorrow' to 'tmrw' and 'ok' to 'I'm on my way', so I no longer know which of us is typing.`
    Passes 1, 2, 5; 4 is tight. 3 is middling — autocorrect-makes-me-say-the-wrong-thing is well
    covered, and "'I'm on my way'" is close to a known meme phrasing.

15. **112** — `My video app mutes me when I laugh and unmutes me mid-sentence to my boss, so I've learned to laugh in writing.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4, 5. 3 is decent, but the mutes-on-laugh gag is a common video-call complaint and
    the boss is a stock character.

16. **120** — `My phone lives at 1% for an hour and dies at 60% in the cold, so I trust neither number and carry a cable like a rosary.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4, 5; 3 good. Rejected at the tie-break: it is still, at bottom, "phone battery bad",
    which the brief lists as an obvious complaint. The 1%/60% inversion is fresh but it is doing
    the work of the whole line.

17. **134** — `The bank app texts a code to the phone I'm holding, to prove I'm the person holding the phone, to show me a balance of eleven dollars.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4 partly. Fails 4: 134 characters for one joke told three times ("the phone I'm
    holding" appears twice) and it duplicates candidate 5's material without beating it.

18. **126** — `My phone's last percent has been holding for forty minutes, which means the other ninety-nine were apparently the freeloaders.`
    Passes 1, 2, 4. 5 is weak — no behaviour beyond the percentage, and 3 is a variation on the
    "last 1% lasts forever" observation that is already a well-known phone joke.

## Explicit rejects

- **Over 200:** `My phone battery is the single worst piece of technology ever made by anyone, and I say
  that as someone who has owned three of them, replaced each one at full price, and still cannot
  get from seven in the morning to lunchtime without hunting for a socket in a train station.`
  — 274 characters. Fails the gate outright; also one sentence doing the work of four.
- **Names no gadget or app:** `Everything is too complicated now and nothing works the way it used to,
  which is why I have stopped trusting gadgets altogether.` — 128. Loses criterion 1 to any line
  that names a device, whatever else it does.
- **Obvious complaint (printer):** `My printer always jams at the worst possible moment, which is
  basically every time I try to print something important.` — 118. This is the brief's own example
  of a reheated roast; no concrete behaviour, no fresh angle.
- **Obvious complaint (battery):** `My phone battery dies way too fast and I have to charge it twice a
  day, which is honestly ridiculous in this day and age.` — 121. "Phone dies fast" with nothing new,
  exactly as flagged in the brief.
- **Crass for its own sake:** `My smartwatch is a piece of s*** and I would throw it in the bin if the
  bin had an app for it.` — 94. The rule is explicit that crudeness for its own sake does not count;
  the epithet replaces the joke rather than setting it up.
- **Known catchphrase:** `I for one welcome our new robot overlords, especially the one in my kitchen
  that keeps reordering milk.` — 103. The first clause is a borrowed meme; an entry that leans on it
  is not original writing, and a judge that recognises it stops reading the rest.

## Winner

**Candidate 1**, 122 characters, before revision:

> My watch counts standing by the arm, not the legs, so I chop onions one-handed and let the other arm hang like an apology.

**Why it beats the runner-up (candidate 2).** Both lines find the same mechanical truth — the stand
ring is measuring a wrist angle, not a body — so criteria 1, 2 and 3 are close. They separate on
criterion 5, the concrete gripe, and then on 4. Candidate 2 stages the discovery in a kitchen with
a counter, which is furniture; candidate 1 stages it in a *pose*: one hand chopping onions while
the free arm is left hanging at the side in penance. That is a thing the writer's body actually
does, and the reader recognises their own kitchen in it. "Apologize" in candidate 2 is the arm
doing something to no one; "hang like an apology" in candidate 1 makes the arm *be* the apology,
which is the joke. Candidate 1 also names the fault in nine words ("counts standing by the arm,
not the legs") where candidate 2 spends a longer clause on a conditional ("won't count standing
with my arm on the counter") — the rule is stated once, cleanly, before the image lands.

## Revision (mandatory, from running the program)

- **Before (122):** `My watch counts standing by the arm, not the legs, so I chop onions one-handed and let the other arm hang like an apology.`
- **After (118):** `My watch counts standing by the arm, not the legs, so I chop onions one-handed, the other arm hanging like an apology.`

**Reason.** Running the entry and reading it on one line rather than in a draft column exposed the
weakest part: "and let the other arm hang". "Let" is a permission verb doing the work of a picture,
and "the other arm" arrives seven words after the arm was last mentioned, so the reader has to
re-attach it to the chopping hand. Cutting "and let" and turning the clause into an absolute
construction — "the other arm hanging like an apology" — puts the hanging arm in the same breath as
the chopping hand, saves four characters, and removes the only verb in the sentence that was not
earning its place. It is also a sharper ending: the line now stops on the image instead of on the
verb that introduces it.

The character count moved 122 → 118; the joke and the target are unchanged, which is the point of a
tightening rather than a rewrite.
